# FINN — TERM REGISTRY
### The words Finn hands the person, and the rules for handing them over.
**September 2026 · v1 · companion to Finn-Insight-Education-Library.md**

---

## PART ONE — THE RULE

**Every term is introduced as something they are about to hear, never as something they should already know.**

Not *"LVR means loan to value ratio."*
But *"Your broker will call this your LVR."*

The reader understands the thing first and is handed the name second. The name arrives as equipment for a conversation they're about to have, not as a gap in their education. This removes shame, gives the word a purpose, and matches the product promise exactly: before you talk to anyone about your finances.

### The five writing rules

**1 · Plain thing first, name second.**
A label, a heading, or a calc result never leads with a term. It leads with the plain question or description. The name attaches afterwards, quietly.

- ❌ `LVR = 60.2%`
- ✅ `How much of the place is borrowed — 60.2%` · then: *Lenders and brokers call this your LVR.*

**2 · Only name a word a professional will actually say to them.**
That is the entire test. If a broker, planner, accountant or lawyer will use the word in the room, Finn hands it over. If not, Finn describes the thing and moves on without naming it. This caps the vocabulary at what is useful rather than what is complete.

**3 · One new term per block, maximum.**
A tile can easily throw five words at someone. Most of them can be described without being named. Where a block would introduce two, one gets named and the other gets described.

**4 · Named once, never re-explained.**
A term introduced on one tile is used bare everywhere after it. The affordance carries the definition instead: a named term renders with a light dotted underline, and tapping it shows its one-liner again. **The definition is available, not present.** This is what lets the session build fluency without building text.

**5 · Every number gets one forward clause on why it matters, never an evaluation.**
"Lenders change their terms at certain levels of this" turns a figure from trivia into something worth asking about. It describes how the industry behaves, never whether their number is good. A clause that would have to change if their number changed is an evaluation, and it does not ship.

### Tone — the thing that decides whether this works

**The industry's language is the subject. The reader's gap never is.**

"Lenders call it." "Nobody explains this." "The number they look at." Finn is translating a system, not correcting a person. This is the same posture as *the part most people don't realise*, extended to vocabulary.

## PART TWO — THE REGISTRY

Each entry carries: the plain description that comes first, the name that follows, the one-liner the glossary shows, who actually says it, and where it is introduced.

Terms are introduced in tile order. A term is named exactly once and used bare thereafter.

### 1 · Equity
**Introduced on** Tile 1 · **Said by** brokers, planners, lenders

| | |
|---|---|
| **Plain first** | What you own of it |
| **Handed over** | This is what everyone means by your equity. |
| **Glossary line** | The part of a property you own outright. Its value, less whatever is still owing on it. |
| **Why it matters** | It's the figure a lender starts from when you ask them for anything. |

---

### 2 · LVR
**Introduced on** Tile 1 · **Said by** brokers, lenders

| | |
|---|---|
| **Plain first** | How much of the place is borrowed |
| **Handed over** | Lenders and brokers call this your LVR, for loan to value ratio. |
| **Glossary line** | What you still owe, as a share of what the property is worth. |
| **Why it matters** | Lenders change the terms they'll offer at certain levels of it, including whether lenders mortgage insurance comes into it. |

---

### 3 · Offset
**Introduced on** Tile 1 · **Said by** brokers, lenders

| | |
|---|---|
| **Plain first** | What interest is charged on |
| **Handed over** | The account doing that is called an offset. |
| **Glossary line** | An everyday account linked to your loan. Whatever sits in it comes off the balance interest is charged on, and you can still spend it any time. |
| **Why it matters** | It's the one loan feature that works without you doing anything, and the one most often left half-used. |

---

### 4 · Redraw
**Introduced on** Tile 3 · **Said by** brokers, lenders

| | |
|---|---|
| **Plain first** | Money you've paid ahead and could pull back out |
| **Handed over** | Lenders call this redraw. |
| **Glossary line** | Extra repayments you've made that the lender may let you take back. Unlike an offset, the money is theirs to control and the terms can change. |
| **Why it matters** | It looks like savings on a statement and behaves differently when you need it. |

---

### 5 · Variable and fixed
**Introduced on** Tile 1 · **Said by** brokers, lenders

| | |
|---|---|
| **Plain first** | Whether your rate can move |
| **Handed over** | A rate that can move is called variable. One that's locked for a period is fixed. |
| **Glossary line** | Variable rates move when the lender moves them. Fixed rates stay put for an agreed term, then revert. |
| **Why it matters** | It decides whether your repayment is a known quantity or a moving one. |

---

### 6 · Take-home pay
**Introduced on** Tile 2 · **Said by** everyone

| | |
|---|---|
| **Plain first** | What actually lands in your account |
| **Handed over** | This is your take-home pay, or net pay. |
| **Glossary line** | What reaches you after tax and anything else taken out before you see it. |
| **Why it matters** | It's the only income figure that tells you what you can actually spend. |

---

### 7 · Marginal rate
**Introduced on** Tile 3 · **Said by** accountants, planners

| | |
|---|---|
| **Plain first** | The rate the next dollar you earn gets taxed at |
| **Handed over** | Accountants call this your marginal rate. |
| **Glossary line** | The tax rate that applies to your next dollar of income, rather than an average across everything you earn. |
| **Why it matters** | Interest you earn is taxed at it. Interest an offset saves you isn't taxed at all. It's why the two aren't comparable on their headline rates. |

---

### 8 · Consolidating
**Introduced on** Tile 4 · **Said by** planners, funds

| | |
|---|---|
| **Plain first** | Bringing accounts together into one |
| **Handed over** | Funds and planners call this consolidating. |
| **Glossary line** | Closing super accounts and moving the balances into one. |
| **Why it matters** | Each account carries its own fees, and closing one can close the insurance held inside it. |

---

### 9 · Income protection
**Introduced on** Tile 5 · **Said by** planners, risk specialists, insurers

| | |
|---|---|
| **Plain first** | Cover that replaces part of your pay while you can't work |
| **Handed over** | This is income protection. |
| **Glossary line** | Pays a monthly amount while you're unable to work, and stops when you return. Different from cover that pays a lump sum. |
| **Why it matters** | It's the cover most households don't hold and the one that matches how their life is actually funded. |

---

### 10 · TPD
**Introduced on** Tile 5 · **Said by** planners, risk specialists, insurers

| | |
|---|---|
| **Plain first** | Cover that pays if you could never work again |
| **Handed over** | Insurers call this TPD, for total and permanent disability. |
| **Glossary line** | A lump sum paid if you're permanently unable to work. A separate event from dying, and separate again from being off work for a while. |
| **Why it matters** | Holding one of the three doesn't cover you for the others. |

---

### 11 · Waiting period
**Introduced on** Tile 5 · **Said by** planners, risk specialists, insurers

| | |
|---|---|
| **Plain first** | How long before a policy starts paying |
| **Handed over** | This is the waiting period. |
| **Glossary line** | The stretch between when you stop working and when a policy begins paying. Often 30, 60 or 90 days. |
| **Why it matters** | It's the gap your own money has to cover, which is what ties cover to your safety net. |

---

### 12 · Binding nomination
**Introduced on** Tile 6 · **Said by** lawyers, planners, funds

| | |
|---|---|
| **Plain first** | Who your super goes to |
| **Handed over** | The form that decides it is called a binding nomination. |
| **Glossary line** | An instruction on your super account naming who receives it. Binding means the fund must follow it. Most expire after three years unless the fund offers a non-lapsing one. |
| **Why it matters** | Your super usually isn't covered by your will. Without a valid nomination the fund's trustee decides. |

---

### 13 · Enduring power of attorney
**Introduced on** Tile 6 · **Said by** lawyers

| | |
|---|---|
| **Plain first** | Someone who can act for you if you can't |
| **Handed over** | The document is an enduring power of attorney. |
| **Glossary line** | Names a person to make decisions for you while you're alive but unable to make them yourself. A will only takes effect after death and doesn't cover this. |
| **Why it matters** | It's the gap most households don't know they have. |

---

### 14 · Deductible
**Introduced on** Tile 7 · **Said by** accountants

| | |
|---|---|
| **Plain first** | An expense you can claim against income |
| **Handed over** | Accountants call this deductible. |
| **Glossary line** | A cost you're allowed to subtract from income before tax is worked out. |
| **Why it matters** | Interest on an investment loan generally is. Interest on the loan for the home you live in isn't. It's why two identical-looking loans behave differently. |

---

### 15 · PAYG instalments
**Introduced on** Tile 9 · **Said by** accountants, the ATO

| | |
|---|---|
| **Plain first** | Paying tax through the year rather than in one hit |
| **Handed over** | The ATO calls these PAYG instalments. |
| **Glossary line** | Quarterly prepayments toward the tax on income no employer withholds for you. |
| **Why it matters** | The year they start, it's common to pay last year's bill and this year's instalments across the same few months. |

## PART THREE — DESCRIBED, NEVER NAMED

These come up but no professional will say them, so they get described and moved past. Naming them spends a term slot for nothing.

| Thing | How Finn refers to it |
|---|---|
| Package / annual fee | "a package the loan charges you for" |
| Lenders mortgage insurance | named only where LVR thresholds are mentioned, never introduced on its own |
| Concessional contributions | never raised at all — advice line |
| Asset allocation | "how it's spread" |
| Cost base | "a record kept for tax, going back to when you bought" |
| Intestacy | "the law has a fixed formula for who gets what" |
| Unsecured | "debts like a credit card or a personal loan" |
| Franking credits | not raised in v1 |

## PART FOUR — BUILD

**Content file.** Each term is an entry in a `terms` registry keyed by id, carrying `plain`, `handover`, `glossary`, `why`, `said_by`, `introduced_on`.

**Markup.** A named term in library copy is wrapped as `<term id="lvr">LVR</term>`. The renderer draws a dotted underline and binds the popover. Copy is authored with the wrapper; the renderer never decides what to mark.

**First use.** On the tile that introduces a term, the `handover` sentence renders after the plain label. Everywhere after, only the marked term renders, with the popover carrying the definition.

**Cap.** The renderer asserts no more than one first-introduction per block. A block that would introduce two is a content error, not a layout one.

**Glossary popover.** Shows `glossary`, then `why`. Never the handover sentence — that's a one-time thing.
